## **2. Work Completed This Month**

### **2.1 Friction-Aware Regime and Slip-Ratio Telemetry**

SUMO does not expose slip ratio directly — it uses friction as an edge attribute that affects the maximum-speed and following-distance behavior of vehicles. The slip ratio was therefore derived from kinematic observables:

slip ≈ |v_actual - v_intended| / v_actual

where v_intended is the IDM equilibrium speed for the current edge density and v_actual is reported by TraCI. On high-friction edges (μ = 0.9) slip is essentially zero (~0.01 noise floor); on low-friction edges (μ = 0.4) slip rises to 0.15–0.25 during accel events. Slip was added to the per-vehicle telemetry dict as a fifth channel.

A low-friction scenario was run: same 3-lane motorway, μ = 0.4 across all edges, 5 simulated minutes (~600 vehicles). Mean slip ratio across the population is 0.12; mean speed dropped from 96 km/h (high-friction reference) to 78 km/h, consistent with the IDM responding to lower friction with longer following distances and lower speeds. Slip was added as a fifth dimension to the gate's context vector. On the low-friction scenario the gate's mixture-weight output is ~42% highway, ~8% urban, ~50% weather — the weather mixture rises substantially in response to elevated slip, exactly as designed.

The hand-picked weather target weights were re-tuned. A per-channel correlation analysis with the slip-ratio observable produced Pearson correlations of 0.71 (w_accel — strongly correlated, accel is a major slip driver), 0.43 (w_wave — wavy steering on slippery roads is dangerous), 0.18 (w_prox — proximity matters but less than acceleration on ice), and 0.04 (w_speed — high speed is a problem on dry surfaces too). The target was adjusted from the original literature-inspired (0.20, 0.30, 0.20, 0.30) to (0.10, 0.40, 0.15, 0.35), and the gate was re-trained against the updated target (loss converges to 0.06).

A bonus result: the heterogeneous 5%-aggressive experiment was re-run on the low-friction scenario. Population mean AI = 49.3; reference low-friction normal = 41.7. Shockwave bump in low-friction = +7.6 vs. the high-friction +6.8. Aggressive drivers are more impactful on slippery roads — kinematic perturbations have higher consequences per unit input on low-grip surfaces. This is consistent with the basic vehicle-dynamics intuition and is reported in the final report as a small extension result.

### **2.2 ROS2 Wrapper**

A colcon workspace was set up at ~/ros2_ws and a new vipp_aggressiveness package created. Two custom message types were defined. VehicleTelemetry.msg carries (speed, accel, prox, wave, jerk, slip, vehicle_id, stamp). AggressivenessIndex.msg carries (ai_score, ai_weather, mixture_weights[3], vehicle_id, stamp). A third type, AggressivenessSummary.msg, carries population-level aggregates (mean_ai, max_ai, n_vehicles, n_affected_neighbors, aggressive_fraction).

AgentNode subscribes to /telemetry, builds the 5-dim context vector on each callback, runs the gate-head agent in inference mode (torch.no_grad(), .eval()), computes AI and AI_Weather via AggressivenessModel, and publishes on /env/aggressiveness_index. The agent is loaded once at construction time. A TelemetryPublisherNode reads a SUMO trajectory log line by line and publishes telemetry messages at the recorded rate, acting as a deterministic replay source for verifying reproducibility.

End-to-end testing on a 60-second telemetry stream produced a publish rate of 14.9 Hz (matching the SUMO step rate within sub-step jitter), median end-to-end latency 18 ms, 99th percentile 41 ms — both within the 50 ms budget. Bag-record/replay confirmed that re-running the AgentNode against replayed telemetry produces bit-identical output (verified by checksum on the AI score field). A unit test that ingests a 100-message slice and checks output equality was added to the test suite.

The summary node (SummaryNode) maintains an in-memory dict keyed by vehicle_id, evicts entries older than 2 seconds, and publishes the population aggregates at exactly 1 Hz on /env/aggressiveness_summary. n_affected_neighbors fluctuates between 8 and 14 in the 5%-aggressive scenario, consistent with the spatial-decomposition results from March.

Multi-vehicle handling. AgentNode was extended to handle multiple vehicle_ids via internal routing. At 50 vehicles the 99th percentile end-to-end latency grows to 78 ms — above the 50 ms budget at high load. Profiling shows that the agent's forward pass dominates the cost (PyTorch overhead at single-sample batches). Documented as a known limitation; can be addressed in future work via small-window batched inference.

## **4. Discussion and Closing Thoughts on the Term**

The work this term split roughly in half between agent-side machine learning (weeks 1–7) and systems / scale work (weeks 8–14). The agent-side work — gate-head architecture, count-based curiosity, Bellman-error tracking — is not novel as machine learning; it is textbook applications of well-understood techniques. The novelty of the project lies in the pipeline as a whole: a closed-form, interpretable Aggressiveness Index that feeds an RL fine-tuning loop, that produces a regime-aware predictor, that publishes on a ROS2 topic that downstream planners can subscribe to. Interpretability survives all the way through the stack. Most autonomous-driving stacks lose interpretability somewhere between perception and planner; ours does not.

The single most consequential decision of the term was the diagnostic-and-fix loop in weeks 5–6 (Bellman-error tracking, state-visitation heatmaps, count-based curiosity). Those diagnostics surfaced the conservative-bias problem the midterm's accuracy curve was hiding, and the fix cascaded into the per-class accuracy improvements, the eventual confidence to ship a final report whose claims are defensible, and the readiness to do the dose-response and Social Latency work that turned out to be the term's central empirical contribution.
