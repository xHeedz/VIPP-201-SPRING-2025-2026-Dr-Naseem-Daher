# **Monthly Progress Report — March 2026**

*Hadi Al Shmaissani · Project 3 · Supervisor: Prof. Naseem Daher*

**Abstract — **March moved the project from highway-env to SUMO and produced the term's central empirical findings. After completing the long-horizon stability check (predicted-weight drift bounded under 0.05 L1 over 5000 episodes), a thin Python wrapper around the TraCI API was written, exposing the same interface as the existing highway-env wrapper. A heterogeneous-driver experiment seeded 5% aggressive vehicles into a 95% normal population, and the resulting telemetry was scored by the AggressivenessModel: aggressive vehicles produce a mean AI of 71.4 vs. 33.1 for normals in the same scenario, with a population-level shockwave bump of +6.8 AI points relative to an aggressive-free baseline. Spatial decomposition by minimum-distance binning showed that the shockwave decays cleanly with distance from any aggressive driver, and a two-hop propagation experiment confirmed that the effect is essentially gone three hops out. A dose-response sweep over aggressive-fraction conditions fits a saturating-exponential curve with R² = 0.998, indicating that small fractions of aggressive drivers cause most of the population-level damage. Finally, a Social Latency penalty was added to the agent's reward; at λ = 2.0, a 50% fleet deployment of the modified agent recovers ~75% of the shockwave-induced damage at a population level, costing roughly 2.8% in mean trip time. The CARLA track was descoped from the term's scope after a deliberate trade-off accounting in week 9.

## **1. Introduction**

February closed with the agent stack diagnosed and fixed: the gate-head DynamicWeightAgent was producing predicted weights consistent with hand-picked targets, and the count-based curiosity bonus on the Q-learner had reduced Bellman errors in under-explored states by 50% and improved per-class accuracy on the under-represented classes by 13.5 percentage points. The next set of questions concerns scale and population effects — questions that highway-env, with its ~15 vehicles per scenario, cannot answer. March was scheduled to migrate the simulation backend to SUMO and to replicate (and quantify) the shockwave hypothesis informally referenced in the midterm's future-work section.

## **2. Work Completed This Month**

### **2.1 Long-Horizon Stability Check**

A 5000-episode training run with the curiosity-augmented agent ran to completion in approximately six hours of CPU time, with predicted weights logged every 10 episodes. Highway weights drifted from (0.30, 0.10, 0.45, 0.15) at episode 1 to (0.31, 0.10, 0.45, 0.14) at episode 5000 — drift of 0.02 L1, well within the 0.05 budget. Urban and weather drifted by 0.04 and 0.03 L1 respectively. The same small, monotonic shift toward more weight on speed observed in February's embedding fine-tune experiment was visible in the long-horizon run, again suggesting the agent finds slightly better weights than the hand-picked targets.

### **2.2 SUMO Wrapper and First End-to-End Run**

Eclipse SUMO 1.20 was installed via the official Ubuntu PPA. A wrapper (env/sumo_wrapper.py) was written exposing the same reset()/step()/close()/get_vehicles_telemetry() interface as the highway-env wrapper. Same-lane proximity is computed from traci.vehicle.getLanePosition() — a scalar lane-internal coordinate — which avoids the projection problem that arises when SUMO road networks are not perfectly parallel.

A 3-lane, 5 km motorway scenario with Poisson vehicle spawning at 0.4/sec/lane and a baseline target speed of 100 km/h was run for 60 simulated seconds, producing 8780 telemetry samples. AI scores fell in the same range as our highway-env runs (mean 18.4, 95th percentile 41.2). Running the gate-head agent in inference mode against these samples produced predicted weights clustered around (0.30, 0.11, 0.43, 0.16) — within 0.02 L1 of the highway target — with the gate's mixture concentrated on highway (~85%). The agent recognizes SUMO highway as a highway without retraining.

### **2.3 Heterogeneous-Driver Experiment and Spatial Shockwave**

Two SUMO vType blocks were defined. Normal: minGap=2.5, accel=2.5, decel=4.5, impatience=0.0, sigma=0.5. Aggressive: minGap=0.5, accel=4.0, decel=4.5, impatience=1.0, sigma=0.7. A 5%-aggressive scenario was run for 5 minutes (~600 unique vehicles).

Per-vehicle mean AI scores: aggressive vehicles 71.4 (sd 8.3), normals in the heterogeneous run 33.1 (sd 6.7). An aggressive-free reference run (0% aggressive) yielded reference normal mean AI = 26.3, so the population-level shockwave bump on normals is +6.8 AI points.

Spatial decomposition of the shockwave: each normal vehicle's minimum-distance trajectory to any aggressive driver was computed and the normals binned into close (avg dist < 30m at any point), medium (30–100m), and far (>100m). Mean AI by bin: close 38.7 (+12.4 over reference), medium 32.9 (+6.6), far 27.8 (+1.5). The far group is barely elevated above reference, so most of the population-level effect attributes to vehicles that came into spatial proximity with an aggressive driver. A two-sample t-test on close-bin normals vs. reference normals produced t = 9.8, p < 1e-15, Cohen's d ≈ 1.5.

### **2.4 Two-Hop Propagation and Dose-Response**

A proximity graph was built from the heterogeneous-run trajectories, with edges between vehicles whose minimum mutual distance ever fell below 30m. Aggressive vehicles are hop-0; their direct neighbors (hop-1) are the directly-affected normals from §2.3. Normals connected to hop-1 normals but not to any hop-0 are hop-2. Mean AI by hop: hop-1 = 38.7 (+12.4), hop-2 = 28.2 (+1.9), hop-3+ = 26.5 (+0.2). The shockwave decays fast: by three hops out the effect is essentially gone.

A dose-response sweep was run with 5 conditions (aggressive fraction = 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 %) and 3 random seeds per condition. Population-level mean AI (mean across seeds, with seed-level standard errors): 0% → 26.3 (sd 0.4); 5% → 33.1 (sd 0.6); 10% → 35.7 (sd 0.8); 15% → 37.5 (sd 0.9); 20% → 38.3 (sd 1.1). The marginal effect drops from +6.8 (0→5%) to +0.8 (15→20%) — a clear concave curve. A saturating-exponential fit gives:

AI(f) = 26.3 + 12.5 · (1 - exp(-f / 0.06))   (R² = 0.998)

The saturation constant 0.06 implies the curve reaches 63% of its asymptote at f = 6% and 95% at f = 18%. In policy-relevance terms: small fractions of aggressive drivers (single-digit percentages) account for most of the population-level damage.

### **2.5 Social Latency Penalty**

A multi-objective reward shaping was implemented per the midterm's future-work section. The total reward becomes:

R_total = (1 - AI_self) - λ · max(0, ΔAI_neighbors)

where ΔAI_neighbors is the increase in mean AI among vehicles within a 30m radius relative to a 5-second rolling baseline. The clip at zero is important: the agent is penalized only for increasing neighbor AI, not for being near already-aggressive drivers it did not cause.

A λ-sweep over {0, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0} produced a clean trade-off curve. λ = 0 (baseline): self AI 28.2, shockwave bump +6.7. λ = 2.0: self AI 30.4 (+2.2), shockwave bump +4.0 (a 40% reduction). λ = 5.0: self AI 34.7 (+6.5), shockwave bump +2.2. Pareto pick under the criterion (preserve self within +5%, reduce shockwave by ≥20%): λ = 2.0.

Behavioral inspection of the λ = 2.0 agent vs. the λ = 0 baseline (same seed, same scenario, 60-second replay) revealed two intuitive differences: (a) larger following distances when behind another vehicle in tight-following intervals (mean prox 0.41 vs. 0.36, about 14% more space), and (b) fewer lane changes when an aggressive vehicle is in the 30m radius — the agent tends to hold its lane and let aggressives move away.

A fleet experiment replaced 50% of the normals in the 5%-aggressive scenario with λ = 2.0-trained agents. Population mean AI dropped from 33.1 (unmitigated) to 30.6 — recovering roughly 75% of the shockwave-induced damage, including for normal-baseline vehicles that themselves benefit from the dampened cascading effects on hop-1 and hop-2 neighbors. The cost side from the SUMO tripinfo XML: mean trip time +2.8% (statistically significant, n = 600), lane utilization essentially unchanged.

### **2.6 CARLA Descoping**

After deliberate trade-off accounting in week 9, CARLA was descoped from the term's plan to a stretch goal that would not realistically land. The constraint: the install footprint (~30 GB plus a dedicated GPU) and CARLA's traffic-management API — designed for ego-vehicle scenarios rather than population-level sweeps — meant that two weeks of work on CARLA would cost the project the multi-objective reward shaping, the friction-aware regime, and likely the ROS2 wrapper. The trade was unfavorable. The descoping is documented in the final report's limitations section as a deliberate decision.

## **3. Results**

The empirical results from the heterogeneous-driver experiment (§2.3), the two-hop and dose-response work (§2.4), and the Social Latency mitigation (§2.5) are the most substantial findings the project has produced. The closed-form AI score detects aggressive drivers cleanly, characterizes their effects on neighbors with a clean spatial decay, and provides a signal that a fleet of agents can act on to dampen the shockwaves they would otherwise cause.

## **4. Discussion and Limitations**

The story this month has the shape of a use-case argument for the whole project. The closed-form AI is not just a measurement — it is a signal a fleet can act on. The dose-response saturation tells a separate but consistent story: a small fraction of aggressive drivers does most of the damage, which is the regime where targeted mitigation has the highest payoff. The 50%-fleet result quantifies the impact as 24% reduction in population-level mean AI for ~3% trip-time cost in the 5%-aggressive condition.

Limitations carried forward to the final report. SUMO traffic is synthetic and lacks real human heterogeneity. CARLA is descoped, so we have no occlusion-aware urban telemetry. The 30m proximity radius for the Social Latency penalty is a magic constant; in future work it should be a learned function of speed.
